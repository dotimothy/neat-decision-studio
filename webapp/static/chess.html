The line it expects

It has not moved yet.

Its own move, then the best reply it found, and so on to the end of its look-ahead. It chooses the move whose worst case is best: this is the line where both sides do what it thinks is best for them.

Depth by depth

It searches one ply deep, then two, and so on, each time trying the last depth's best move first: a good first move is what lets the pruning cut the rest short. A depth that runs out of time is dropped.

The moves it weighed

 

In the order it tried them. The first move searched gets a full score and sets the bar. Each later move is searched only until it is shown not to clear the bar, so most have no score, only the most they could be worth, and cost a fraction of the positions: that is alpha-beta pruning at work. A move that clears the bar becomes the new best.

How it counts the position

All its judgement is this sum, in pawns. A pawn is 1, a knight 3.2, a bishop 3.3, a rook 5, a queen 9. Placement is by rule: knights and bishops toward the centre, pawns forward (more in the centre, much more on the seventh rank), rooks on the seventh rank and the middle files, the king in its corner while the queens are on and in the centre once they are off.

Its method

  1. Minimax. It looks a fixed number of plies ahead and assumes each side plays what is best for itself; a position's worth is what the side to move can be sure of. (Written as negamax: one routine, the sign flipped each ply.)
  2. Alpha-beta pruning. It carries two bounds down the tree: what it can already be sure of, and what the opponent can already hold it to. Once a reply is found that makes a move worse than one already in hand, the move's other replies are not looked at. The answer is the same as full minimax; the positions are far fewer.
  3. Move ordering. The last depth's best line first, then captures of the biggest piece by the smallest, then promotions. Pruning cuts most when the best move is tried first.
  4. Iterative deepening. Depth 1, then 2, up to the depth chosen, within eight seconds a move.
  5. Quiescence. At the end of the look-ahead it does not score the position as it stands: it plays captures out (up to four more plies), so it never counts a piece it is about to lose.
  6. Endings. Checkmate is worth more than any material, and a sooner mate more than a later one. Stalemate, bare kings and a third repetition (looked for right after its own move) are worth nothing.
  7. What it does not have. Nothing learned, no opening book, no memory of positions it has already searched, and no notion of pawn structure or king safety beyond the placement rules. It is JavaScript on chess.js's rules, a few thousand positions a second, where a native engine searches millions.
